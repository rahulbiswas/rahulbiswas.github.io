The Big Picture: Momentum vs. Impulse

What is Momentum (p)? — "Inertia in Motion"

In the previous unit, inertia (mass m) measured how hard it is to start or stop an object in general. Momentum (symbol p) measures how hard it is to stop a moving object. It depends on two factors: how much stuff is moving (mass m) and how fast it is moving (velocity v).

Momentum = mass × velocity  ⟹  p = m · v
The 3 Formula Rearrangements:
• Find momentum: p = m · v
• Find velocity: v = pm
• Find mass:     m = pv
Units & Vector Rule:
• Mass m in kg (1 kg = 1,000 g)
• Velocity v in m/s
• Momentum p in kg·m/s
• Vector: Always include direction if given (e.g. 6 kg·m/s west)!
5 Classic Momentum Traps on the Test:
  • At Rest = Zero Momentum: A huge 10,000 kg truck parked on the side of the road has p = 0 kg·m/s because v = 0. A child on rollerblades moving at 1 m/s has more momentum than a parked truck!
  • Supertanker vs. Bullet: An object can have huge momentum either from a huge mass moving slowly (supertanker) OR a tiny mass moving at huge velocity (bullet).
  • Equal Momentum Condition: When can a 1,000 kg car and a 2,000 kg truck have the same momentum? When the lighter 1,000 kg car travels at twice the velocity of the 2,000 kg truck (e.g., 1000 × 20 = 2000 × 10).
  • Moon vs. Earth Trap (HW9 #5): A lunar vehicle tested at 10 km/h on Earth and 10 km/h on the Moon has the EXACT SAME momentum! Mass m and velocity v do not depend on gravity g.
  • Scaling Trap (Study Guide #22): If you double velocity, momentum doubles (2×). If you triple BOTH mass and velocity (3m × 3v), momentum becomes 3 × 3 = 9 times greater!

What is Impulse? And How Does It Come from Newton's 2nd Law?

To change an object's momentum, you have to push or pull on it with a Force (F) over a Time interval (t). The product F · t is called Impulse.

Mrs. Swaminathan tests how the Impulse–Momentum Theorem is derived directly from Newton's 2nd Law (F = ma):

F = m · a = m · Δvt   ⟹   F · t = Δ(mv)  (Impulse = Change in Momentum)
Crucial Exam Distinction — Don't Mix These Up!
  • Does a moving object possess impulse? NO! A moving object possesses momentum (mv). It does not possess impulse; impulse only happens while a force acts over time to change momentum.
  • Impulse = Change in Momentum (Δmv), NEVER momentum itself!
  • Impulse (F · t) vs. Impact Force (F): Impulse is force multiplied by time (in N·s or kg·m/s); impact force is just the push F (in Newtons).

The 3 Impulse–Momentum Cases (F · t = Δmv)

Case 1: Increasing Momentum (Want Maximum Δmv ⟹ Big F × Big t)

To give a ball or cannonball the largest possible velocity boost, you apply the largest force possible for the longest time possible:

  • Following through in baseball, tennis, or golf: Extending contact time t while swinging hard (big F × big t) maximizes impulse and gives the ball a huge change in momentum.
  • Long Cannon vs. Short Cannon (HW9 #8): For the same explosive gas force F, a long-barreled cannon imparts a greater speed to the cannonball than a short cannon because the force acts on the ball for a longer time t inside the longer barrel!

Case 2: Decreasing Momentum Over a Long Time (Small F × Big t)

Suppose your car is out of control and you must stop by hitting either a haystack or a concrete brick wall.

The Haystack vs. Wall Rule (Memorize This Template!): Whether you hit a haystack or a brick wall, your momentum drops from mv to 0, so the change in momentum (Δmv) is the SAME and the impulse (F · t) is the SAME! Hitting the haystack is safer because it lengthens the time of impact (t), which reduces the impact force (F). (If time t increases by 100×, force F decreases by 100×!)

All 10 "Cushioning / Long Time t" Questions on the Study Guide & HW9 Use This Exact Same Answer:

Scenario on Test / HW9 How It Increases Time (t) to Reduce Force (F)
1. Catching a fast baseball Moving your hand backward with the ball extends stopping time t, reducing the sting/force F on your hand.
2. Bending knees when jumping Bending your knees extends stopping time 10–20× compared to a stiff-legged landing, slashing the force on your bones.
3. Car crumple zones, airbags & padded dashboards Crumpling metal and inflating airbags lengthen the time Δt over which the car/passenger stops, greatly reducing collision force F.
4. Boxer "rolling/riding with the punch" Moving the head back with the glove increases contact time t. If a boxer triples contact time (3t), the force is reduced to 1/3!
5. Gym mats, carpets, wooden dance floors & safety nets Mats, carpets, wooden floors, and nets "give" (yield), increasing impact time t so the stopping force F is much smaller than on concrete/hardwood.
6. Nylon climbing ropes & bungee cords Stretching under tension increases stopping time t so the climber/jumper is brought to a halt by a small, safe average force F.
7. Egg tossed into a sagging sheet vs. wall Both stop the egg (same Δmv and impulse), but the sheet stretches out stopping time t so the force F stays too small to crack the shell.

Case 3: Decreasing Momentum Over a Short Time (Big F × Small t)

Sometimes you want a huge force—or a short stopping time causes a dangerous force:

  • Taekwondo / Karate brick break: The expert strikes the bricks at high speed and stops her hand in a tiny fraction of a second (small t). Because t is very small, the impact force F on the bricks is enormous, snapping the bricks!
  • Boxer moving into a punch: If a boxer leans forward into a punch and cuts contact time in half (½t), the impact force is doubled (2F)!
  • Bare fist & 6-oz gloves vs. 16-oz boxing gloves (HW9 MT2 #5–6): A bare fist (or thinner 6-oz glove) has much less padding than a 16-oz glove, so the punch stops in a shorter time t, delivering a larger impact force F.

Bouncing & The Pelton Water Wheel

Why Bouncing Requires Roughly TWICE the Impulse of Stopping

Because momentum is a vector (direction matters!), reversing an object's direction requires two steps:

  1. Impulse #1 to bring the moving object from +mv to a dead stop (0): magnitude = mv.
  2. Impulse #2 to throw it back in the opposite direction from 0 to −mv: another mv!
Dead Stop: |Δp| = mv    vs.    Stop + Bounce Back: |Δp| = 2mv (Twice the Impulse!)
  • The 3-Way Baseball Question (HW9 MT2 #7): Between (1) catching a ball, (2) throwing a ball from rest, and (3) catching a ball and throwing it back at the same speed, choice (3) catching AND throwing it back has the greatest change in momentum (2mv vs. mv)!
  • Flowerpot on Your Head: A flowerpot that bounces off your head delivers twice the impulse (and hurts much more) than one that breaks/stops without bouncing.
  • Pelton Wheel (Gold Rush Application): Old water wheels had flat paddles that merely stopped the water (impulse = mv). Lester Pelton designed curved cup-shaped paddles so the water stream made a U-turn and bounced off in the opposite direction—doubling the impulse (2mv) transferred to the wheel!

Conservation of Momentum, Collisions & Recoil

Why Momentum Is Conserved (Connection to Newton's 3rd Law)

The Law of Conservation of Momentum states: In the absence of an external force, the total momentum of a system remains unchanged (ptotal, before = ptotal, after).

Why? It comes directly from Newton's 3rd Law (Action–Reaction)! Whenever two objects collide (whether a small car & heavy truck, a taekwondo hand & wooden block, or your hand hitting a wall with 100 N for 2 s):

The 4 "Equal & Opposite" Collision Rules (Tested Repeatedly!): 1. Force (F): Equal in magnitude and opposite in direction on both objects.
2. Time of impact (t): Exact same contact duration for both objects.
3. Impulse (F · t): Equal in magnitude and opposite in direction on both objects.
4. Change in Momentum (Δmv): Equal in magnitude and opposite in direction! What Object 1 loses (−75 kg·m/s), Object 2 gains (+75 kg·m/s), so the sum p1 + p2 never changes!

Three Types of Collision & Recoil Problems

Collision Type Definition & Formula Classic Test Examples
1. Elastic Collision Objects rebound without lasting deformation or generation of heat.
m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂' 		• Billiard balls: A 1 kg ball at 2 m/s hits a resting 1 kg ball; Ball 1 stops (0 m/s) and Ball 2 rolls away at 2 m/s.
• Gas molecule collisions.
2. Inelastic (Coupled) Collision Objects do not rebound (characterized by deformation and/or heat) and stick/lock together as one combined mass (m1 + m2):
m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁ + m₂) · v_f 		• Two railroad boxcars locking couplers
• Moving car locking bumpers with parked car
• Bullet embedding in a wooden block
• 10 kg snake swallowing a 1 kg frog
3. Explosion / Gun & Cannon Recoil Both start at rest, so total momentum before = 0 and total momentum after = 0!
m_bullet · v_bullet = M_gun · V_gun 		• 0.025 kg bullet fired at 230 m/s from a 0.9 kg gun.
• Why is the rifle's recoil speed so small? Equal & opposite momentum change (m·v = M·V), but the rifle's mass M is much larger!
• Equal recoil speed? Make cannon & ball equal mass.
• Bullet heavier than gun? The gun recoils faster than the bullet!
Head-On Vector Trap (Study Guide Conceptual #29): "What is the combined momentum of two objects, each having a momentum of 3 kg·m/s, moving toward each other?"
Because momentum is a vector, one has +3 kg·m/s and the other has −3 kg·m/s. Their combined net momentum is (+3) + (−3) = 0 kg·m/s!

vgun = 5.75 kg·m/s0.9 kg ≈ 6.39 m/s.
v_gun ≈ 6.39 m/s

HW9 True/False (All 10) & Key Fill-in-the-Blanks

HW9 Question 7 — All 10 True/False Statements Explained

Statement T / F Why?
1. Momentum is not equal to the mass of an object divided by its velocity. TRUE Correct—momentum is mass multiplied by velocity (p = mv), not divided!
2. The momentum of an object can change. TRUE Whenever a net force acts over time (impulse), velocity changes, so momentum changes.
3. Two objects with the same mass will always have the same momentum. FALSE Only if they also have the exact same velocity (speed and direction)!
4. All moving objects don't have momentum. FALSE Every moving object has mass and velocity, so every moving object has momentum.
5. When an object speeds up, it gains momentum. TRUE Since p = mv, increasing speed v increases the magnitude of momentum.
6. Objects with different masses can't have the same momentum. FALSE They can! A 1,000 kg car at 20 m/s has the same momentum as a 2,000 kg truck at 10 m/s.
7. Direction does not matter when you are measuring momentum. FALSE Momentum is a vector quantity—direction always matters!
8. Momentum can be transferred from one object to another. TRUE In collisions (like billiard balls), momentum transfers from the first ball to the second.
9. When objects collide, some momentum is lost. FALSE Total momentum is conserved in all collisions (elastic and inelastic) when no external forces interfere!
10. A tiny bullet can have more momentum than a huge truck. TRUE Yes—if the huge truck is at rest (p = 0) or creeping ultra-slowly while the bullet moves very fast!
