The Golden Equation: Slope-Intercept Form

\[ y = mx + b \]

\(m = \text{slope} = \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\)   ·   \(b = y\text{-intercept}\), where the line crosses the \(y\)-axis at \((0, b)\)

Recipe 1 · Identify \(m\) and \(b\) First solve for \(y\) so the equation is in \(y = mx + b\) form. The number multiplying \(x\) is the slope \(m\); the constant term (keep its sign!) is the \(y\)-intercept \(b\).
Recipe 2 · Write from Slope \(m\) & Point \((x_1, y_1)\) Plug \(m\), \(x_1\), and \(y_1\) into \(y = mx + b\) and solve for \(b\). Then substitute \(m\) and \(b\) back into \(y = mx + b\).
Recipe 3 · Parallel Lines (\(m_1 = m_2\)) Nonvertical lines are parallel if and only if they have the same slope (\(m_1 = m_2\)) and different \(y\)-intercepts (\(b_1 \neq b_2\)). To write a parallel line through a point, copy \(m\) and solve for the new \(b\).
Recipe 4 · Write from Two Points \((x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2)\) Step 1: Compute \(m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\).
Step 2: Plug \(m\) and either point into \(y = mx + b\) to find \(b\).
Step 3: Write \(y = mx + b\).

The 3 Ways to Graph a Linear Equation (Lesson 4.4)

Method When to Use It Quick Steps
1. Table of Values Any equation (especially standard form like \(2x + y = 4\)) Pick 3 clean \(x\)-values (use multiples of the denominator if \(m\) is a fraction!), find each \(y\), plot the 3 points, and draw a straight line with arrows.
2. Slope \(m\) & \(y\)-Intercept \(b\) Equation is in (or easily rewritten in) \(y = mx + b\) form Plot \((0, b)\) on the \(y\)-axis first. From \((0, b)\), count \(\text{rise}\) and \(\text{run}\) to plot a 2nd point (and a 3rd to double-check), then connect with arrows.
3. Slope \(m\) & Any Point \((x_1, y_1)\) You are given a slope \(m\) and one point that is not on the \(y\)-axis Plot \((x_1, y_1)\) first. From that point, move by \(\text{run}\) horizontally and \(\text{rise}\) vertically to land on a 2nd point, then draw the line with arrows.

4 Classic Test Traps to Avoid

Trap 1 · Swapped Terms & Minus Signs In \(y = 3 - \frac{1}{4}x\), rewrite as \(y = -\frac{1}{4}x + 3\) first! The slope is \(m = -\frac{1}{4}\) (not \(3\)!) and \(b = 3\) (not \(-\frac{1}{4}\)!).
Trap 2 · Divide EVERY Term When Solving for \(y\) From \(2y = 6x - 5\), dividing by \(2\) gives \(y = 3x - \frac{5}{2}\). Never leave the constant \(-5\) undivided!
Trap 3 · Parallel Needs Different \(b\) Same slope \(m\) and same \(y\)-intercept \(b\) means the two equations describe the exact same line, not two separate parallel lines!
Trap 4 · Rise Over Run (Not Run Over Rise!) \(m = \frac{\text{rise (vertical, }\Delta y\text{)}}{\text{run (horizontal, }\Delta x\text{)}}\). For \(m = \frac{2}{3}\), go up 2, right 3 — never up 3, right 2!

Write an Equation of a Line in Slope-Intercept Form

You have learned that the slope-intercept form of an equation of a line is:

\[ y = mx + b \]

Given the slope, \(m\), and the \(y\)-intercept, \(b\), of a line, you can substitute these values directly into \(y = mx + b\) to write an equation of the line.

When given an equation in another form, you can add, subtract, multiply, or divide both sides to write an equivalent equation in slope-intercept form — isolating \(y\) on the left side — so you can read off \(m\) and \(b\) right away.

Example 7 Use slope-intercept form to identify slopes and \(y\)-intercepts p. 154

An equation of a line is given. State the slope and \(y\)-intercept of the line.

\[ y + 2x - 6 = 0 \]

Solution

First write the equation in slope-intercept form.

\[ \begin{aligned} y + 2x - 6 &= 0 && \\ y + 2x - 6 + 6 &= 0 + 6 && \text{Add } 6 \text{ to both sides.} \\ y + 2x &= 6 && \text{Simplify.} \\ y + 2x - 2x &= 6 - 2x && \text{Subtract } 2x \text{ from both sides.} \\ y &= -2x + 6 && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \end{aligned} \]

Comparing the equation \(y = -2x + 6\) with \(y = mx + b\):

  • Slope: \(m = -2\)
  • \(y\)-intercept: \(b = 6\)
Think Math Paula notices that the ordered pair \((0, 6)\) is a solution of \(y = -2x + 6\). Is this ordered pair also a solution of \(y + 2x - 6 = 0\)? Is any ordered pair \((x, y)\) that is a solution of \(y = -2x + 6\) also a solution of \(y + 2x - 6 = 0\)? Why or why not?

Yes to both questions!

Check \((0, 6)\) in \(y + 2x - 6 = 0\):

\[ 6 + 2(0) - 6 = 6 + 0 - 6 = 0 \quad \checkmark \]

More generally, any ordered pair \((x, y)\) that solves \(y = -2x + 6\) also solves \(y + 2x - 6 = 0\). Adding \(6\) to both sides and subtracting \(2x\) from both sides produces equivalent equations, which always have the exact same solution set (and represent the exact same line).

For each line, state its slope and its \(y\)-intercept.

#1 \(5x + 4y = 8\)

First write the equation in slope-intercept form:

\[ \begin{aligned} 5x + 4y &= 8 && \\ 5x + 4y - 5x &= 8 - 5x && \text{Subtract } 5x \text{ from both sides.} \\ 4y &= -5x + 8 && \text{Simplify.} \\ \frac{4y}{4} &= \frac{-5x + 8}{4} && \text{Divide both sides by } 4. \\ y &= -\frac{5}{4}x + 2 && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \end{aligned} \]

Comparing \(y = -\frac{5}{4}x + 2\) with \(y = mx + b\):

Slope: \(m = -\dfrac{5}{4}\),   \(y\)-intercept: \(b = 2\).

#2 \(2x - 3y = 7\)

First write the equation in slope-intercept form (following the textbook's steps):

\[ \begin{aligned} 2x - 3y &= 7 && \\ 2x - 3y + 3y &= 7 + 3y && \text{Add } 3y \text{ to both sides.} \\ 2x &= 7 + 3y && \text{Simplify.} \\ 2x - 7 &= 7 + 3y - 7 && \text{Subtract } 7 \text{ from both sides.} \\ 2x - 7 &= 3y && \text{Simplify.} \\ \frac{2x - 7}{3} &= \frac{3y}{3} && \text{Divide both sides by } 3. \\ y &= \frac{2}{3}x - \frac{7}{3} && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \end{aligned} \]

Comparing \(y = \frac{2}{3}x - \frac{7}{3}\) with \(y = mx + b\):

Slope: \(m = \dfrac{2}{3}\),   \(y\)-intercept: \(b = -\dfrac{7}{3}\).

#3 \(5y - x = 15\)
\[ \begin{aligned} 5y - x &= 15 && \\ 5y - x + x &= 15 + x && \text{Add } x \text{ to both sides.} \\ 5y &= x + 15 && \text{Simplify.} \\ \frac{5y}{5} &= \frac{x + 15}{5} && \text{Divide both sides by } 5. \\ y &= \frac{1}{5}x + 3 && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \end{aligned} \]

Slope: \(m = \dfrac{1}{5}\),   \(y\)-intercept: \(b = 3\).

#4 \(2y - 3x = -4\)
\[ \begin{aligned} 2y - 3x &= -4 && \\ 2y - 3x + 3x &= -4 + 3x && \text{Add } 3x \text{ to both sides.} \\ 2y &= 3x - 4 && \text{Simplify.} \\ \frac{2y}{2} &= \frac{3x - 4}{2} && \text{Divide both sides by } 2. \\ y &= \frac{3}{2}x - 2 && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \end{aligned} \]

Slope: \(m = \dfrac{3}{2}\),   \(y\)-intercept: \(b = -2\).

#5 \(6y + 5x = 24\)
\[ \begin{aligned} 6y + 5x &= 24 && \\ 6y + 5x - 5x &= 24 - 5x && \text{Subtract } 5x \text{ from both sides.} \\ 6y &= -5x + 24 && \text{Simplify.} \\ \frac{6y}{6} &= \frac{-5x + 24}{6} && \text{Divide both sides by } 6. \\ y &= -\frac{5}{6}x + 4 && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \end{aligned} \]

Slope: \(m = -\dfrac{5}{6}\),   \(y\)-intercept: \(b = 4\).

#6 \(3y + 4x = 3\)
\[ \begin{aligned} 3y + 4x &= 3 && \\ 3y + 4x - 4x &= 3 - 4x && \text{Subtract } 4x \text{ from both sides.} \\ 3y &= -4x + 3 && \text{Simplify.} \\ \frac{3y}{3} &= \frac{-4x + 3}{3} && \text{Divide both sides by } 3. \\ y &= -\frac{4}{3}x + 1 && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \end{aligned} \]

Slope: \(m = -\dfrac{4}{3}\),   \(y\)-intercept: \(b = 1\).

Example 8 Write an equation of a line given its slope and \(y\)-intercept p. 156

Use the given slope and \(y\)-intercept of a line to write an equation in slope-intercept form.

a) Slope, \(m = \dfrac{1}{4}\);   \(y\)-intercept, \(b = 3\)

Solution

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \\ y &= \frac{1}{4}x + 3 && \text{Substitute the given values for } m \text{ and } b. \end{aligned} \]

b) Slope, \(m = -2\);   \(y\)-intercept, \(b = -5\)

Solution

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \\ y &= -2x + (-5) && \text{Substitute the given values for } m \text{ and } b. \\ y &= -2x - 5 && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]
Think Math If you graphed the equations in Example 8, which line would go upward from left to right? Which would go downward from left to right? Which line would be steeper? Explain.
  • Upward from left to right: \(y = \dfrac{1}{4}x + 3\), because its slope \(m = \dfrac{1}{4}\) is positive.
  • Downward from left to right: \(y = -2x - 5\), because its slope \(m = -2\) is negative.
  • Steeper line: \(y = -2x - 5\) is steeper because the absolute value of its slope is greater: \(\lvert -2 \rvert = 2 > \left\lvert \dfrac{1}{4} \right\rvert = \dfrac{1}{4}\).

Parallel Lines Have Equal Slopes

Lines in the coordinate plane that have the same slope but different \(y\)-intercepts are always parallel — they never intersect.

Key Rule for Parallel Lines

Two distinct lines \(y = m_1 x + b_1\) and \(y = m_2 x + b_2\) are parallel if and only if:

\[ m_1 = m_2 \quad \text{and} \quad b_1 \neq b_2 \]

For example, you can predict from their equations alone that these two lines are parallel because both have slope \(m = \dfrac{2}{3}\) while their \(y\)-intercepts (\(2\) and \(5\)) are different:

\[ y = \frac{2}{3}x + 2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad y = \frac{2}{3}x + 5 \]
Example 9 Write an equation of a line given its \(y\)-intercept and a parallel line p. 157

Write an equation.

An equation of a line is \(2y = 6 - 3x\). Write an equation of a line parallel to this given line that has a \(y\)-intercept of \(6\).

Solution

First write the given equation in slope-intercept form.

\[ \begin{aligned} 2y &= 6 - 3x && \\ \frac{2y}{2} &= \frac{6 - 3x}{2} && \text{Divide both sides by } 2. \\ y &= 3 - \frac{3}{2}x && \text{Simplify.} \\ y &= -\frac{3}{2}x + 3 && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \end{aligned} \]

The given line has slope \(m = -\dfrac{3}{2}\) and \(y\)-intercept \(b = 3\).

Then write an equation for the parallel line with slope \(m = -\dfrac{3}{2}\) and \(y\)-intercept \(b = 6\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= -\frac{3}{2}x + 6 && \text{Substitute the values for } m \text{ and } b. \end{aligned} \]

So, an equation of the line with a \(y\)-intercept of \(6\) and parallel to \(2y = 6 - 3x\) is \(y = -\dfrac{3}{2}x + 6\).

Think Math Give an example of another equation for a line that is parallel to the two lines in Example 9.

Any line with the same slope \(m = -\dfrac{3}{2}\) and a different \(y\)-intercept (any number other than \(3\) or \(6\)) is parallel to both lines.

For example: \(y = -\dfrac{3}{2}x + 1\),   \(y = -\dfrac{3}{2}x - 4\),   or   \(y = -\dfrac{3}{2}x\).

Write an Equation of a Line Given Its Slope and a Point on the Line

You can also find an equation of a line if you are given the slope and any point on the line — even when the point is not the \(y\)-intercept!

For example, the line shown below has a slope of \(m = \dfrac{2}{5}\) and passes through the point \((2, 3)\). You know the slope, but you do not yet know the \(y\)-intercept.

Math Note (Graph Check)

From the graph, you can estimate that the line intersects the \(y\)-axis somewhere between \(2\) and \(3\). Let's solve algebraically to find the exact \(y\)-intercept!

STEP 1: Substitute the given slope and the \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates of the given point into the equation \(y = mx + b\).

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= \frac{2}{5}x + b && \text{Substitute the slope, } \frac{2}{5}\text{, in the equation.} \\ 3 &= \frac{2}{5}(2) + b && \text{Substitute } 3 \text{ for } y \text{ and } 2 \text{ for } x \text{ in the equation.} \\ 3 &= \frac{4}{5} + b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

STEP 2: Solve the equation to find the \(y\)-intercept \(b\).

\[ \begin{aligned} 3 - \frac{4}{5} &= \frac{4}{5} + b - \frac{4}{5} && \text{Subtract } \frac{4}{5} \text{ from both sides.} \\ \frac{11}{5} &= b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

Notice that \(\dfrac{11}{5} = 2.2\), which matches our visual estimate between \(2\) and \(3\)!

STEP 3: Use the given slope and the value for \(b\) to write an equation for the line.

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= \frac{2}{5}x + \frac{11}{5} && \text{Substitute the values of } m \text{ and } b. \end{aligned} \]
Example 10 Write an equation of a line given its slope and a point on the line p. 159

Write an equation.

A line has slope \(-5\) and passes through the point \((1, -8)\). Write an equation of the line.

Why We Can Substitute \((1, -8)\)

Because \((1, -8)\) lies on the line, \(x = 1\) and \(y = -8\) are a solution of the equation.

Solution

First use the given slope, \(-5\), and the values \(x = 1\) and \(y = -8\) to find the \(y\)-intercept.

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \text{Write the slope-intercept form.} \\ -8 &= -5(1) + b && \text{Substitute the values for } m, x, \text{ and } y. \\ -8 &= -5 + b && \text{Simplify.} \\ -8 + 5 &= -5 + b + 5 && \text{Add } 5 \text{ to both sides.} \\ -3 &= b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

The \(y\)-intercept is \(-3\).

Then use the given slope, \(-5\), and the \(y\)-intercept, \(-3\), to write an equation in slope-intercept form.

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \text{Write the slope-intercept form.} \\ y &= -5x + (-3) && \text{Substitute the values for } m \text{ and } b. \\ y &= -5x - 3 && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

So, an equation of the line is \(y = -5x - 3\).

#10 A line has slope \(-3\) and passes through the point \((-6, 8)\). Write an equation of the line.

Substitute \(m = -3\), \(x = -6\), and \(y = 8\) into \(y = mx + b\) to find \(b\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \\ 8 &= -3(-6) + b && \text{Substitute } m = -3, x = -6, y = 8. \\ 8 &= 18 + b && \text{Multiply.} \\ 8 - 18 &= 18 + b - 18 && \text{Subtract } 18 \text{ from both sides.} \\ -10 &= b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

Substitute \(m = -3\) and \(b = -10\) into \(y = mx + b\):

Answer: \(y = -3x - 10\).

#11 A line has slope \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) and passes through the point \((0, 1)\). Write an equation of the line.

Because the point \((0, 1)\) has \(x = 0\), it is the \(y\)-intercept itself! So \(b = 1\). (You can also confirm by substituting \(m = \frac{1}{3}, x = 0, y = 1\):)

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \\ 1 &= \frac{1}{3}(0) + b && \text{Substitute } m = \frac{1}{3}, x = 0, y = 1. \\ 1 &= b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

Answer: \(y = \dfrac{1}{3}x + 1\).

#12 A line has slope \(2\) and passes through the point \((1, 5)\). Write an equation of the line.

Substitute \(m = 2\), \(x = 1\), and \(y = 5\) into \(y = mx + b\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \\ 5 &= 2(1) + b && \text{Substitute } m = 2, x = 1, y = 5. \\ 5 &= 2 + b && \text{Multiply.} \\ 5 - 2 &= 2 + b - 2 && \text{Subtract } 2 \text{ from both sides.} \\ 3 &= b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

Answer: \(y = 2x + 3\).

Write an Equation of a Line Given a Point on the Line and the Equation of a Parallel Line

An equation for the red line shown in the graph is \(y = \dfrac{3}{4}x + 1\).

The blue line is parallel to the red line and passes through the point \((2, 3)\). Because the blue line and the red line are parallel, they have the same slope, \(m = \dfrac{3}{4}\).

Now we know the slope of the blue line and a point \((2, 3)\) on it. Substitute those into \(y = mx + b\) and solve for \(b\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \\ y &= \frac{3}{4}x + b && \text{Substitute the slope } \frac{3}{4} \text{ in the equation.} \\ 3 &= \frac{3}{4}(2) + b && \text{Substitute } 3 \text{ for } y \text{ and } 2 \text{ for } x \text{ in the equation.} \\ 3 &= \frac{3}{2} + b && \text{Multiply.} \\ 3 - \frac{3}{2} &= \frac{3}{2} + b - \frac{3}{2} && \text{Subtract } \frac{3}{2} \text{ from both sides.} \\ \frac{3}{2} &= b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

So, the equation of the blue line is \(y = \dfrac{3}{4}x + \dfrac{3}{2}\) (or \(y = \dfrac{3}{4}x + 1.5\)).

Math Note (Graph Check)

From the graph, you can see that the blue line intersects the \(y\)-axis at \(1.5\). So the \(y\)-intercept is \(1.5 = \dfrac{3}{2}\).

Example 11 Write an equation given a point and a parallel line p. 161

Write an equation.

A line passes through the point \(\left(\dfrac{1}{4}, 8\right)\) and is parallel to the line represented by the equation \(y = 2 - 4x\). Write the equation of the line.

Strategy

Use the given equation to find the slope of the parallel line.

Solution

First write the equation \(y = 2 - 4x\) in slope-intercept form.

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= 2 - 4x && \\ y &= -4x + 2 && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \end{aligned} \]

The given line has slope \(m = -4\). So, the line parallel to \(y = 2 - 4x\) also has slope \(m = -4\).

Then write the equation of the line that passes through \(\left(\dfrac{1}{4}, 8\right)\) and has slope \(-4\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \\ 8 &= -4\left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + b && \text{Substitute the values for } m, x, \text{ and } y. \\ 8 &= -1 + b && \text{Multiply.} \\ 8 + 1 &= -1 + b + 1 && \text{Add } 1 \text{ to both sides.} \\ 9 &= b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

The \(y\)-intercept is \(9\).

So, the equation of the line is \(y = -4x + 9\).

Think Math If you graphed the parallel lines \(y = 2 - 4x\) and \(y = -4x + 9\) on the same coordinate plane, would the graph of \(y = -4x + 9\) lie above or below the graph of \(y = 2 - 4x\)? Explain.

The graph of \(y = -4x + 9\) would lie above the graph of \(y = 2 - 4x\).

Rewriting \(y = 2 - 4x\) as \(y = -4x + 2\), both lines have the same slope \(m = -4\), so they stay parallel. Because \(9 > 2\), the \(y\)-intercept of \(y = -4x + 9\) is higher — in fact, for every value of \(x\), \(y = -4x + 9\) is exactly \(9 - 2 = 7\) units above \(y = -4x + 2\).

Two-Step Recipe from Two Points

You can also find an equation of a line if two points on the line are given:

  1. Find the slope \(m\) using the slope formula:
    \[ m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} \]
  2. Find the \(y\)-intercept \(b\) by substituting \(m\) and the \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates of either given point into \(y = mx + b\), then solve for \(b\).
  3. Write the equation by substituting \(m\) and \(b\) into \(y = mx + b\).
Math Note

You can substitute the values of \(m\), \(x\), and \(y\) of either point into \(y = mx + b\) to find the \(y\)-intercept — both points give the exact same value of \(b\)! Using the second point is also a great way to check your answer.

Example 12 Write an equation of a line given two points on a line p. 163

Write an equation of the line that passes through the pair of points \((1, 3)\) and \((2, -4)\).

Solution

First use the slope formula to find the slope. Let \((1, 3)\) be \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((2, -4)\) be \((x_2, y_2)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Slope} &= \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} && \text{Use the slope formula.} \\ &= \frac{(-4) - 3}{2 - 1} && \text{Substitute values.} \\ &= \frac{-7}{1} && \text{Subtract.} \\ &= -7 && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

The line has slope \(m = -7\).

Method 1 — Use the slope \(m = -7\) and the point \((1, 3)\) to find the \(y\)-intercept.

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \\ 3 &= -7(1) + b && \text{Substitute the values for } m, x, \text{ and } y. \\ 3 &= -7 + b && \text{Multiply.} \\ 3 + 7 &= -7 + b + 7 && \text{Add } 7 \text{ to both sides.} \\ 10 &= b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

Method 2 — Use the slope \(m = -7\) and the point \((2, -4)\) to find the \(y\)-intercept.

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= mx + b && \text{Write in slope-intercept form.} \\ -4 &= -7(2) + b && \text{Substitute the values for } m, x, \text{ and } y. \\ -4 &= -14 + b && \text{Multiply.} \\ -4 + 14 &= -14 + b + 14 && \text{Add } 14 \text{ to both sides.} \\ 10 &= b && \text{Simplify.} \end{aligned} \]

Both methods give the exact same \(y\)-intercept: \(b = 10\).

So, an equation of the line is \(y = -7x + 10\).

Three Ways to Sketch a Linear Graph

Every linear equation \(y = mx + b\) has a graph that is a straight line. A linear equation has an infinite number of solutions, and every ordered pair \((x, y)\) that makes the equation true lies on the line. Depending on what information you are given, you can pick any of these three methods:

Method What You Need How It Works
1. Table of Values Any linear equation Pick three \(x\)-values, calculate their \(y\)-values, plot the three points, and connect them with a ruler.
2. Slope \(m\) & \(y\)-Intercept \(b\) Equation in \(y = mx + b\) form Plot \((0, b)\) on the \(y\)-axis, then count \(\dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}}\) to plot a second point.
3. Slope \(m\) & a Point Slope \(m\) and any point \((x_1, y_1)\) Plot \((x_1, y_1)\), then count \(\dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}}\) to plot a second point.

Method 1: Understand Graphing Linear Equations (Table of Values)

Let's graph the equation \(y = \dfrac{1}{2}x + 1\) step by step.

STEP 1 Construct a table of values. Choose three values for \(x\) and solve to find the corresponding values for \(y\).

Smart Tip — Pick Friendly \(x\)-Values Choose values of \(x\) that give integer values of \(y\)! Because the slope is \(\dfrac{1}{2}\) (denominator \(2\)), multiples of \(2\) work cleanly:
  • When \(x = 2\): \(\quad y = \dfrac{1}{2}(2) + 1 = 1 + 1 = 2\)
  • When \(x = 4\): \(\quad y = \dfrac{1}{2}(4) + 1 = 2 + 1 = 3\)
  • When \(x = 6\): \(\quad y = \dfrac{1}{2}(6) + 1 = 3 + 1 = 4\)
\(x\) \(2\) \(4\) \(6\)
\(y\) \(2\) \(3\) \(4\)

STEP 2 Plot the three points \((2, 2)\), \((4, 3)\), and \((6, 4)\) on the coordinate plane, and draw a straight line through them with a ruler.

Think Math Can you draw the graph with just two points? Why is it a good idea to include a third point when you graph an equation?

Yes — geometrically, two points are enough to determine a unique straight line.

Why include a third point? The third point acts as a built-in error check! If you make an arithmetic or plotting mistake with one of the points, the three points will not line up on a single straight line. When all three points fall along the same ruler edge, you know your calculations and plotting are accurate.

Graphing Directly from Slope-Intercept Form

\(y = \underbrace{m}_{\text{slope} = \frac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}}}x + \underbrace{b}_{y\text{-intercept }(0, b)}\)

One fast way to graph an equation in slope-intercept form is to use the \(y\)-intercept \(b\) to plot a starting point \((0, b)\) on the \(y\)-axis, and then use the slope \(m = \dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}}\) to step to a second point on the line:

  1. Plot \((0, b)\) on the \(y\)-axis.
  2. Use the slope \(m = \dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}}\) (or any equivalent fraction) to move vertically by the rise and horizontally by the run to find a second point.
  3. Draw a straight line through the points with a ruler.
Example 14(a) Graph \(y = x + 2\) using \(m\) and \(b\) p. 167

a) Graph the equation \(y = x + 2\). Use \(1\) grid square on both axes to represent \(1\) unit for the \(x\) interval from \(0\) to \(4\), and the \(y\) interval from \(2\) to \(6\).

STEP 1 The equation \(y = x + 2\) has \(y\)-intercept \(b = 2\), so it passes through the point \((0, 2)\). Plot \((0, 2)\) on the \(y\)-axis.

STEP 2 The slope of the line is \(m = 1\), so the ratio \(\dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}} = 1\).

\[ \text{Slope} = 1 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \frac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}} = \frac{1}{1} = \frac{2}{2} = \frac{3}{3} = \frac{4}{4} = \ldots \]

Using \(\dfrac{4}{4}\), move up \(4\) units from \((0, 2)\) to \((0, 6)\), and then right \(4\) units to plot the point \((4, 6)\).

Tip — Equivalent Slope Fractions You can use any convenient integer values for the rise and run as long as the ratio \(\dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}} = 1\). Here, using \(4\) for both the rise and the run places the two points farther apart, which makes lining up your ruler even more accurate!
Math Note The line graph contains all the points \((x, y)\) whose values of \(x\) and \(y\) make the equation true. Only some of those points can be seen on this graph. So, you can choose any convenient points with integer values for \(x\) and \(y\).

STEP 3 Use a ruler to draw a straight line through \((0, 2)\) and \((4, 6)\). This line is the graph of \(y = x + 2\).

Example 14(b) Graph \(y = -\dfrac{1}{2}x - 3\) using \(m\) and \(b\) p. 168

b) Graph the equation \(y = -\dfrac{1}{2}x - 3\). Use \(1\) grid square on both axes to represent \(1\) unit for the \(x\) interval from \(0\) to \(4\), and the \(y\) interval from \(-5\) to \(0\).

STEP 1 Plot a point on the \(y\)-axis. The equation \(y = -\dfrac{1}{2}x - 3\) has \(y\)-intercept \(b = -3\), so it passes through \((0, -3)\). Plot \((0, -3)\).

STEP 2 Use the slope to find another point on the graph. The slope is \(m = -\dfrac{1}{2}\), so:

\[ \frac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}} = \frac{-1}{2} = \frac{1}{-2} = \frac{-2}{4} = \frac{2}{-4} = \ldots \]

Using \(\dfrac{-2}{4}\), move down \(2\) units from \((0, -3)\) to \((0, -5)\), and then right \(4\) units to plot \((4, -5)\).

Tip — Any Equivalent Rise/Run Works You can use any two integers for the rise and run as long as \(\dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}} = -\dfrac{1}{2}\). For example, using rise \(= -1\) and run \(= 2\) lands on \((2, -4)\), and using rise \(= -2\) and run \(= 4\) lands on \((4, -5)\) — both lie on the exact same line!

STEP 3 Use a ruler and draw a straight line through the points. This line is the graph of \(y = -\dfrac{1}{2}x - 3\).

Graphing from Any Point \((x_1, y_1)\) and Slope \(m\)

You can graph a line even when you are not given the \(y\)-intercept — all you need is one point on the line and the slope \(m\):

  1. Plot the given point \((x_1, y_1)\).
  2. Use the slope \(m = \dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}}\) to step vertically and horizontally from \((x_1, y_1)\) to find a second point. (If moving right goes off your grid, rewrite \(\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{-a}{-b}\) and step in the opposite direction!)
  3. Draw a straight line through the points using a ruler.
Example 15 Graph a line given \(m\) and a point p. 169

Graph a line with slope \(4\) that passes through the point \((3, 2)\). Use \(1\) grid square on both axes to represent \(1\) unit for the interval from \(0\) to \(6\).

STEP 1 Plot the given point \((3, 2)\).

STEP 2 The slope of the line is \(4\), so the ratio \(\dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}} = 4\).

\[ \text{Slope} = 4 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \frac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}} = \frac{4}{1} = \frac{8}{2} = \frac{12}{3} = \frac{16}{4} = \ldots \]

Using \(\dfrac{4}{1}\), start at \((3, 2)\), move up \(4\) units to \((3, 6)\), and then over \(1\) unit to the right to plot \((4, 6)\).

STEP 3 Use a ruler and draw a straight line through \((3, 2)\) and \((4, 6)\). This is the line with slope \(4\) passing through \((3, 2)\). (Its equation is \(y = 4x - 10\).)

Use graph paper. Use \(1\) grid square to represent \(1\) unit on both axes for the \(x\) interval from \(-2\) to \(2\), and the \(y\) interval from \(0\) to \(10\).

#4 Graph a line with slope \(-2\) that passes through the point \((2, 2)\).

Step 1 — Plot the given point: Plot \((2, 2)\).

Step 2 — Use the slope \(m = -2\) to stay inside the grid: Since \(x = 2\) is already at the right edge of \(-2 \le x \le 2\), rewrite the slope with a negative run:

\[ m = -2 = \frac{-2}{1} = \frac{2}{-1} = \frac{4}{-2} \]
  • From \((2, 2)\), move up \(2\) units and left \(1\) unit to plot \((1, 4)\).
  • Continue the pattern to also check \((0, 6)\) and \((-2, 10)\).

Step 3 — Draw the line through \((-2, 10)\), \((0, 6)\), \((1, 4)\), and \((2, 2)\). (Equation: \(y = -2x + 6\).)

#5 Graph a line with slope \(2\) that passes through the point \((-2, 1)\).

Step 1 — Plot the given point: Plot \((-2, 1)\).

Step 2 — Use the slope \(m = 2 = \dfrac{2}{1}\):

  • From \((-2, 1)\), move up \(2\) units and right \(1\) unit to plot \((-1, 3)\).
  • Continue the pattern to also check \((0, 5)\) and \((2, 9)\) inside \(-2 \le x \le 2\), \(0 \le y \le 10\).

Step 3 — Draw the line through \((-2, 1)\), \((-1, 3)\), \((0, 5)\), and \((2, 9)\). (Equation: \(y = 2x + 5\).)

Math Journal (#9–#10)

#9 · Math Journal Graph the equation \(y = 2 - \dfrac{2}{3}x\). Explain how to use the graph to find other solutions of the equation.

Part 1 — Graph the equation: Rewrite as \(y = -\dfrac{2}{3}x + 2\), so \(m = -\dfrac{2}{3}\) and \(b = 2\).

  1. Plot the \(y\)-intercept \((0, 2)\).
  2. Use \(\dfrac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}} = \dfrac{-2}{3}\): move down \(2\) and right \(3\) to \((3, 0)\), and again to \((6, -2)\).
  3. Stepping backwards (\(\text{up }2, \text{left }3\)) gives \((-3, 4)\). Draw the line through the points.

Part 2 — How to find other solutions from the graph: Every single point \((x, y)\) that lies on the graphed line is a solution of \(y = 2 - \dfrac{2}{3}x\). To find another solution, pick any point on the line — especially where the line crosses grid intersections — and read its \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates. For example, reading along the line shows \((-6, 6)\), \((-3, 4)\), \((0, 2)\), \((3, 0)\), and \((6, -2)\) are all solutions, and even non-integer points on the line like \((1.5, 1)\) are solutions too!

#10 · Math Journal Martha says that the point \((4, -2)\) lies on the graph of the equation \(y = -\dfrac{1}{4}x - 1\). Explain how you can find out if she is right without actually graphing the equation.

How to check without graphing: A point \((x, y)\) lies on the graph of an equation if and only if its coordinates make the equation a true statement. Substitute \(x = 4\) and \(y = -2\) into the equation \(y = -\dfrac{1}{4}x - 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y &\stackrel{?}{=} -\frac{1}{4}x - 1 \\ -2 &\stackrel{?}{=} -\frac{1}{4}(4) - 1 \\ -2 &\stackrel{?}{=} -1 - 1 \\ -2 &= -2 \quad \checkmark \end{aligned} \]

Conclusion: Because substituting \(x = 4\) and \(y = -2\) produces a true statement (\(-2 = -2\)), Martha is right — the point \((4, -2)\) lies on the graph of \(y = -\dfrac{1}{4}x - 1\).

#15 · Math Journal Spot the Mistake!

Suppose that Emily shows you some of her homework:

Emily's Homework Problem Graph the equation \(y = -2x + \dfrac{1}{2}\).

Describe Emily's mistake. Graph the equation correctly.

#15 Solution Show Emily's mistakes and the correct graph of \(y = -2x + \dfrac{1}{2}\)
Every Mistake Emily Made
  1. She swapped the slope \(m\) and the \(y\)-intercept \(b\): In \(y = -2x + \dfrac{1}{2}\), the slope is \(m = -2\) and the \(y\)-intercept is \(b = \dfrac{1}{2}\). Emily used \(-2\) as the \(y\)-intercept and plotted \((0, -2)\) instead of \(\left(0, \dfrac{1}{2}\right)\), and she drew a line sloping upward instead of downward (\(m = -2 < 0\)).
  2. She also mis-plotted and reversed the coordinates of her second point: Her second dot is plotted at \((6, 3)\) — \(6\) units right and \(3\) units up — yet she labeled it \((3, 6)\) (swapping \(x\) and \(y\)). Moreover, neither \((6, 3)\) nor \((3, 6)\) satisfies \(y = -2x + \dfrac{1}{2}\) (at \(x = 3\), \(y = -5.5\); at \(x = 6\), \(y = -11.5\)), and her drawn line actually has slope \(\dfrac{3 - (-2)}{6 - 0} = \dfrac{5}{6}\), not \(-2\) (and not even \(\dfrac{1}{2}\)!).

How to graph \(y = -2x + \dfrac{1}{2}\) correctly:

  1. Identify \(m\) and \(b\): Slope \(m = -2 = \dfrac{-2}{1}\) and \(y\)-intercept \(b = \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.5\).
  2. Plot the \(y\)-intercept: Plot \((0, 0.5)\) halfway between \(0\) and \(1\) on the \(y\)-axis.
  3. Use the slope \(m = \dfrac{-2}{1}\):
    • From \((0, 0.5)\), move down \(2\) units and right \(1\) unit to \((1, -1.5)\), and again to \((2, -3.5)\).
    • Going backwards (\(\text{up }2, \text{left }1\)) gives \((-1, 2.5)\) and \((-2, 4.5)\).
  4. Draw a straight line through these points with a ruler.
